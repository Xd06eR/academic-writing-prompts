# Anti-AI-Tell Catalogues

Patterns that mark prose as machine-generated. Load before generating prose and when revising it. The purpose is writing that reads as written by a person: clear, specific, varied. This file improves writing quality; it does not game AI detectors, and no rule here outranks a real clarity, precision, or support requirement.

Why AI prose is detectable: a model writes by statistical likelihood, choosing the option that serves the most readers and subjects. Human writing aims at one reader and one subject, and it shows: the choices come out uneven and particular. Each pattern below is that default choice in one of its forms. Vocabulary habits rotate with every model release; structural habits persist, so structure leads this file. A tell's weight tracks how rarely a careful writer picks it on purpose; the safeguard is several tells together, because any single one can be on purpose.

Standing rules:

- Descriptive, not prescriptive. Clustering and repetition are the signal; a single instance may be fine, except in §2 and §7, which act on one sighting.
- Skip quotations, titles, proper names, and passages that mention a phrase as a phrase rather than use it. Salutations and sign-offs predate chatbots.
- Keep details that carry the writer's voice: a specific unusual detail, mixed feelings stated honestly, a dated reference, a first-person choice the writer can explain, a genuine aside or self-correction. Strip the tells but keep the person: the result must still sound like someone, not something.
- Discipline exemption: standard terminology is exempt (robust in statistics, landscape in geography, paradigm in philosophy of science). Read lists literally; a flagged word's synonyms are not flagged.
- Over-correction is a defect. Do not damage sound prose to satisfy a pattern.
- Register stays academic, per the principles file (`writing-principles.md`). Blog-register advice (casual voice, direct reader address) does not apply here.
- The writer under-detects their own tells. Auditing your own draft calls for a fresh-context pass by a reader who has not seen the draft.
- A supplied writing sample outranks every pattern below; match its voice.

## 1. Vocabulary

Flagged words, with plain replacements:

| Flagged | Replace with |
|---|---|
| delve | examine, investigate, analyze |
| leverage | use, employ |
| foster | promote, develop |
| garner | gather, receive |
| showcase | demonstrate, present |
| underscore (verb) | emphasize, reinforce |
| navigate (non-literal) | manage, address |
| tapestry, realm (abstract) | system, domain, field |
| landscape (abstract) | field, domain, situation |
| intricate, intricacies | complex, detailed |
| multifaceted, holistic, synergy | name the actual structure |
| nuanced (vacuous) | subtle, qualified, fine-grained |
| comprehensive (unjustified) | thorough, extensive |
| robust (non-statistical) | reliable, rigorous |
| pivotal, crucial, vital, key (adjective) | name why it matters, or cut |
| meticulous, enduring, vibrant, profound, valuable | cut, or state the fact |
| cornerstone, testament | basis, evidence |
| paradigm (non-philosophy) | framework, model |
| streamline, cutting-edge, groundbreaking | simplify, recent, novel |
| embark | begin, undertake |
| utilize | use |
| boasts | has |
| deep dive | analysis |
| gate, gating (figurative) | name the actual mechanism |
| certainly, various, numerous | cut, or name the items |
| harness | use, employ |
| interplay | name the interaction |
| enhance, highlight, bolster | name the change |
| additionally | also, or cut |

A formal word outside this list is not a tell by itself.

Copula dodge: replacing plain is/are/has with grander verbs. serves as, stands as, functions as, operates as, marks, represents mean is; features, offers, maintains mean has; refers to opens encyclopedia articles. Restore the plain verb unless the longer one earns its place. The dodge exists because repetition pressure pushes away from basic verbs; resist it.

Gravitas words: fundamental, crucial, essential, pivotal, vital beefing up ordinary claims. Delete the word; if the sentence loses nothing, it was carrying nothing.

Magic adverbs: quietly, deeply, fundamentally, remarkably, arguably, simply, actually, truly, inevitably, inherently, interestingly, importantly, crucially. They lend ordinary content an air of significance without adding a fact.

Business jargon: navigate challenges, unpack, lean into, game-changer, double down, moving forward, circle back, take a step back, on the same page. Replace with plain verbs: handle, explain, commit, significant, next, return, reconsider, aligned.

Clichéd idioms: smoking gun, perfect storm, move the needle, at the end of the day, double-edged sword, tip of the iceberg.

Invented concept labels: coined compound terms wielded as if established (the X paradox, the acceleration trap, workload creep). They name a thing and skip the argument; several in one piece is a strong signal. Name the phenomenon plainly or do not name it.

Lazy extremes: every, always, never, everyone, nobody doing vague work. Sweeping claims need evidence behind them (see the principles file); as a prose habit, specifics beat sweeps.

## 2. Staging instead of stating

The five strongest patterns. A single sighting justifies an edit.

1. **Negative parallelism**: not just X but Y, not X but Y, X rather than Y, not because X but because Y, the cross-sentence reframe ("The question isn't X. The question is Y."), the clipped tail (..., no guessing). The negated half denies a claim nobody made, which makes the positive half sound bigger than it is. Default budget: at most one, absent a reason. State the point directly; keep a contrast only when the reader actually holds the belief being negated.
2. **Engineered emphasis**: one-line closers and restatement paragraphs ("That is the real win."), the repeated closer after several sections, post-example lessons ("This shows the importance of..."), fragment rows ("No aesthetic prior. No nostalgia."), countdown reveals ("Not a bug. Not a feature. A design flaw."), self-answered drama ("The result? Devastating."), ALL CAPS and dotted emphasis ("every. single. day."). Cut what repeats; keep a short sentence only when it carries a fact or a consequence the example does not itself supply.
3. **Sayings that sound deep**: at its core, the real question is, what really matters, the heart of the matter, X is the Y of Z, the language of, the currency of, in reality, the deeper issue, X becomes a trap, the architecture of. These dress an ordinary point as a hidden truth; answer with the specific claim instead.
4. **Staged run-up**: let's dive in, let's explore, here's what you need to know, honestly?, look, the thing is, real talk, heads up, quick note. The opener performs candor rather than making the point. Delete the run-up itself, not merely its tone; a casual honestly inside a sentence is ordinary; the standalone opener is the tell.
5. **Arguing with no one**: this isn't about, I'm not saying, to be clear, don't get me wrong, some might say... but, a tempting approach would be, you might think... but. The text answers an objection that appears nowhere else, usually a remnant of an earlier draft. Cut the defense; retain an objection the text genuinely engages. Rhetorical setups belong here too: what if, here's what I mean, think about it, and that's okay.

## 3. Rhythm by rule

Shapes applied everywhere, whether or not the meaning asks. Weak alone; count these in clusters.

- **Tricolon compulsion**: ideas arriving in threes to sound complete, stretched to four or five. Check that each item adds a distinct idea; merge examples or develop the strongest. Two items beat three; keep three only when the meaning has three parts.
- **Anaphora abuse and repeated openings**: consecutive identical sentence openings. Combine the sentences, lead with the verb, or shift the subject between them. Do not ban the repeated word; deliberate repetition for rhythm is human.
- **Listicle in a trench coat**: "The first... The second... The third...", "The second takeaway is..." wearing prose costume. Write the list or write the prose.
- **Numbered phase labels in prose**: Phase 1, Step 2, Stage 3 where flow by topic belongs.
- **Short punchy fragments as default cadence**: one thought per sentence with no carry-over between them, engineered emphasis paragraphs. Real drafting does not produce this rhythm.
- **Staccato and artificial revelation**: "X. And Y. And Z.", "[Noun]. That's it. That's the [thing].", "This unlocks something. [Word]."
- **Questions answered immediately**: a self-posed question resolved in the same breath. Let a real question breathe or cut it.
- **Paragraphs ending punchily every time**: vary endings.

## 4. Inflation and borrowed authority

The fact underneath is usually sound; keep it and remove the dressing. Weak alone; count in clusters.

- **Inflated significance**: stands as a testament, a pivotal moment, plays a key role, marking or shaping the, reflects a broader, enduring legacy, setting the stage for, evolving landscape, indelible mark, a step in the right direction. The pattern runs from a single phrase up through a stock challenges-and-outlook section to a send-off paragraph ("The future looks bright."). Keep the fact, drop the significance, end on the last concrete fact.
- **Grandiose stakes**: every topic raised to world-historical weight ("will define the next era"). Calibrate stakes to evidence.
- **Historical analogy stacking**: rapid name-checks of companies or revolutions to borrow authority; a technical-writing staple.
- **Dead metaphor**: one metaphor hammered five to ten times across a piece.
- **Promotional register**: sales and travel-guide prose, adjective stacks (a robust, scalable, elegant solution), breathtaking, stunning, renowned, nestled, in the heart of, commitment to, diverse array. Describe the thing plainly.
- **Vague attribution**: experts argue, observers have cited, industry reports, some critics, several publications, coverage lists, follower counts. Attribute the claim to a named source or cut it. In academic prose an unnamed empirical gesture is also an Evidence-Use failure (see the principles file).
- **False profundity**: the truth is simple, history is clear, the real story is..., declared rather than demonstrated. A point that must be announced as clear usually is not.
- **False exclusivity**: the part nobody talks about, for well-known points.
- **False vulnerability**: polished, risk-free performed candor ("And yes, I'm openly in love with..."). Genuine uncertainty is concrete and costs the writer something.
- **Fake casual quotes**: performed casualness in scare quotes.
- **Compliment sandwich**: praise before criticism by formula. State the flaw directly.
- **Superficial -ing riders**: trailing participial phrases attached to a plain fact to make it sound more significant (highlighting its importance, reflecting broader trends, contributing to the development of). Keep the fact; keep the rider only when the source supports what it claims.
- **Quotables**: any sentence engineered to be quoted out of context. Recast it as substance.

## 5. Vague connection and false agency

- **Vague connection**: associated with, in connection with, linked to, tied to, saying two things are connected without saying how. Give the relationship as the source states it; where the source stays silent, leave the connection vague rather than inventing a specific role.
- **False agency**: inanimate things given human verbs, which hides the actor. The decision emerges, the data tells us, the market rewards, the spreadsheet decides, the metric insists. Name the actor when the actor is knowable and it aids the reader. Discipline conventions govern what counts as knowable; established academic constructions (this study shows, the model achieves) are conventions, not tells.
- **Narrator-from-a-distance**: nobody designed this, people tend to, this is why, the lecturer's overview voice. In academic prose this surfaces as sweeping generalization about populations and processes; prefer the specific, attributed claim (see the principles file).

## 6. Hollow transitions and teacher mode

- **Here's the thing / kicker / deal**, here's what most people miss, here's where it gets interesting: false suspense before an unremarkable point.
- **It's worth noting family**: it's worth noting, it bears mentioning, importantly, interestingly, notably as sentence openers. They open points without tying them to what precedes.
- **Throat-clearing openers**: in today's [X], in the modern era, in a world where, when it comes to, it turns out, the uncomfortable truth is, the real X is, this matters because, make no mistake, here's why that matters, full stop, let that sink in, read that again, I'll say it again, I'm going to be honest. State the point; drop the frame.
- **Teacher-mode hand-holding**: let's break this down, let's unpack, let's dive in, let's explore. Assume the reader's competence.
- **Condescending analogy**: think of it as, it's like a..., imagine a world where...; the analogy usually clouds more than it clarifies.
- **Signposted conclusion**: template-style in conclusion announcements; readers sense an ending without being told.
- **Fractal summaries**: preview plus recap at section, subsection, and document level ("And so we return to where we began.").
- **Heading repeated in the first sentence**: let the heading do the work.
- **Writing about the document**: the table below compares, method narration. Describe the subject itself; a caveat stays only when it changes what the reader does next.
- **Despite its challenges formula**: acknowledge-then-dismiss, closing on optimistic boilerplate (despite these challenges, ... continues to thrive).
- **One-point dilution**: one thesis restated N ways with rotating metaphors, for far more words than the point needs.
- **Content duplication**: whole paragraphs or sections repeated verbatim, the mark of text nobody edited.
- **Re-explaining what the reader knows**: background the reader already has, rebuilt before the point.
- **False ranges**: from X to Y where the endpoints share no spectrum; dressed up as a span, it is a two-item list.

## 7. Meta-commentary and chat residue

Remove these outright; nothing here needs rewriting.

- **Chatbot residue**: great question, of course, certainly, I hope this helps, let me know, would you like..., here is a... It is the surest tell in this catalogue, and easy to overlook because it wraps genuine content: strip the wrapper, keep the substance.
- **Knowledge-limit disclaimers**: while details are limited, based on available information, not publicly available, it is believed that, likely grew up, maintains a low profile. Say what the source does not establish, or cut the sentence. Never pad the silence with a guess that merely sounds plausible.
- **Document meta-commentary**: hint, plot twist, spoiler, you already know this, but that's another post, X is a feature not a bug, the rest of this essay explains, in this section we will, as we'll see (the roadmap exception lives in the principles file).
- **Telling instead of showing**: this is genuinely hard, this is what leadership looks like, actually matters. Demonstrate or cut.
- **Vague declaratives**: the reasons are structural, the implications are significant, the stakes are high, the consequences are real. Give the specific thing or cut the sentence.
- **Performative emphasis**: creeps in, I promise, they exist I promise. Manufactured sincerity.

## 8. Punctuation and typography

- **Em dash**: house ceiling is one per 200 words. Where two claims are being connected, prefer a colon, period, or parentheses; a dash avoids committing to how the two clauses relate. Chains of dashes are always a tell. If a supplied writing sample uses dashes, match its rate.
- **Curly quotes**: straight quotes in body text; direct quotes from sources keep their original punctuation. Weak alone; venue typesetting conventions outrank.
- **Semicolons**: keep only where the linked clauses are closely related and the link is easy to follow; split an overloaded sentence when a period states the relation more clearly.
- **Bold as decoration**, including bold-first bullets fronted by a keyword on every item.
- **Unicode decoration**: arrows and ornamental characters a typist cannot produce (→, smart quotes used as decoration).
- **Decorative headings**: emoji, arrows, title case where the convention is sentence case, headings that chase an effect instead of naming the section's contents.
- **Inline-header vertical lists** where prose belongs.

## 9. Sentence shape

- **Metronome lengths**: sentences marching at an even short length, no variation. Vary length with meaning: short for emphasis, long for relationships.
- **Three consecutive sentences of matching length**: break one.
- **Stacked qualifiers**: could potentially, might arguably, to be fair, it's also possible, piled until every claim sounds uncertain. A qualifier stays only when the evidence warrants it; scope statements, safety notices, and genuine corrections remain. Perhaps and tends to are ordinary human hedges, not tells.
- **Wh- starters as crutch**: sentences opening What, When, Why, How as a habitual frame ("What makes this hard is..."). Recast the sentence to open with the subject or the specific constraint.
- **Paragraphs opening with So; sentences opening with Look,**: start with content.
- **Copula avoidance as habit**: see vocabulary; is/are/has are usually the right verbs.
- **Passive voice and missing subjects** where naming the actor aids the reader (discipline conventions govern; see the principles file).
- **Hyphenated pairs everywhere**: hyphenate the compound modifier before the noun (a high-quality report); after the noun the hyphen drops (the report is high quality); dictionary-set compounds (third-party, cross-functional) keep their hyphen in every position.
- **Hedging disguised as reassurance**: "Not always. Not perfectly." as a paragraph voice.

## 10. How to apply

While drafting, these are constraints applied at the point of writing: choose the specific verb, the honest qualifier, the length the meaning needs, the structure the content asks for. Keep a sentence only when the reader gains something from it.

While revising, work in passes:

1. **Mark**. Read the text once through and flag every pattern found, starting with the strongest; work at paragraph scale too, not only sentence scale: a contrast divided between two sentences, three parallel examples, or one closer reused after every section is one tell seen at a larger scale.
2. **Rewrite**. Retain every claim the source supports. Restructure freely, but introduce no fact, name, number, date, quote, or citation beyond what the source material provides; if a sentence needs a detail you do not have, ask or write a simpler sentence.
3. **Re-scan**. The patterns likeliest to survive a rewrite: negative parallelism, one-line closers, tricolons, em dashes, bold decoration. Check also whether the rewrite added or dropped any fact.
4. **Finish**. Restate each point in natural wording instead of patching flagged phrases one by one; when a sentence stays awkward, rebuild the paragraph around its main point. Alternate short and long sentences; real writing does.

Quick checks before delivering (one sighting each, except where the home section sets its own threshold): any throat-clearing opener; any not-X-but-Y contrast against the §2 budget of one; any stacked qualifiers; three consecutive sentences of one length; punchy paragraph endings as a habit, not a single one; any em dash beyond the house ceiling; any vague declarative; any bold decoration; any chatbot wrapper. Name the pattern in any defect report, with location and a concrete substitute.

The standard throughout: prose that reads as one person writing to another, uneven and specific and honest.
