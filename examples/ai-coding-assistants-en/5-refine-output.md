## Revised Draft

Last semester I built a side project of roughly 8,000 lines almost entirely by accepting suggestions from Copilot and Claude Code, and when it broke in production three weeks later I could not read my own code. The bug was a race condition between two background workers, and tracing it required exactly what I had skipped: a mental model of how the workers scheduled themselves, why the message queue was structured the way it was, and which calls held a database connection across an `await` boundary. I had typed almost none of that code, and I had not deliberated about any of it. The experience pointed to something that the productivity literature on AI coding assistants is not measuring.

Generative AI coding assistants harm junior developers' acquisition of debugging skill by displacing the productive struggle that builds the mental models debugging depends on. The harm is acquisition-stage-specific and extends Bainbridge's (1983) ironies of automation. This essay defends three sub-claims: debugging depends on a mental model built through effortful engagement during construction; AI coding assistants short-circuit that effort by replacing authorship with an acceptance decision; and the harm is sharper for juniors than seniors because seniors verify AI output against an existing mental model while juniors accept output in lieu of building one. The strongest counterargument — that Copilot raises task-completion productivity — is conceded, and answered by distinguishing task-completion productivity from skill acquisition. The first piece of the argument is about what debugging actually requires.

Debugging is the activity in which a developer's mental model of a system is most heavily taxed, because the bug exists precisely where the model and the reality diverge. To locate a bug, the developer holds a working representation of the code's intended behaviour, compares it against observed behaviour, and searches the gap; without a working representation, there is nothing to compare against and search degrades into trial. Such representations are not acquired automatically. Ericsson, Krampe, and Tesch-Römer (1993) argue that expert performance is built through deliberate practice — effortful, focused work at the edge of current ability with feedback — rather than through accumulated exposure or hours-on-task. Their account predicts that a developer who has watched another agent write code, even an artificial one, has not acquired the internal representations that authoring the same code would have produced. Cognitive load theory gives this mechanism precise form. Sweller (1988) distinguishes intrinsic load (inherent to the material) from extraneous load (imposed by presentation) and germane load (effortful processing that builds schemas), and argues that durable skill is acquired through germane load. The relevance is direct. The struggle to decide which data structure to use, why one function call belongs inside the transaction and another outside, and how the error path should propagate — the small, effortful authorship decisions that AI assistance can resolve in one autocomplete — are exactly the operations that, in Sweller's framework, constitute germane load. If the mental model is built by effortful construction, then anything that reduces that effort during construction has a price.

AI coding assistants replace the construction-phase effort with an acceptance decision, and the acceptance decision is far less cognitively loaded than authorship. Vaithilingam et al. (2022) report that developers using Copilot often accept suggestions without fully understanding them, and that incorrect suggestions are typically discovered at runtime rather than during code review. The pattern is intelligible in cognitive-load terms: reading a suggestion for surface plausibility imposes a fraction of the germane load that writing the suggestion would have imposed. Bainbridge (1983) anticipates the general structure of this trade. Her argument, framed for process-control engineering, is that automating part of a task degrades the human operator's skill on the un-automated part, because operators no longer practise the manual mode and must intervene precisely when the automation fails — that is, under the hardest conditions. Translated to software development, the un-automated part is debugging, the manual mode is authorship, and the moment of intervention is the production incident. The cost of this short-circuit is not evenly distributed across developers.

Bainbridge's (1983) ironies of automation are sharper when the human in the loop is still acquiring the skill the automation is replacing, because a senior verifies AI output against an existing mental model while a junior accepts AI output in lieu of building one. This is a theoretical extension of Bainbridge and is not, in the available literature, a directly tested claim; it should be read as a contribution requiring empirical follow-up rather than a settled finding. The mechanism is straightforward: verification requires a referent, and seniors have one. Their existing internal representations of how data flows, where state lives, and which abstractions are typically over- or under-fitted to the problem allow them to read an AI suggestion and ask whether it is consistent with those representations. Juniors do not yet hold representations of sufficient fidelity, and so the acceptance decision proceeds against a referent that is itself partially borrowed from the AI's output. Empirical evidence consistent with this account is accumulating. Prather et al. (2024) report that high-performing novice programmers gain from generative AI assistance while low-performing novices fall further behind, suggesting that AI assistance tends to widen rather than narrow skill differences during early acquisition. Their finding does not directly test the verification-versus-substitution mechanism proposed here, but is consistent with it, though it does not on its own rule out alternative mechanisms. What this looks like from the inside is worth describing directly.

From the inside, the experience is not that the AI made me worse at programming — it is that I never built the model in the first place. During the construction of my side project, every interaction with Copilot felt productive: the code worked, the tests passed, and the pull request landed. The deficit was invisible during construction because the artefact being produced was correct; it appeared only when correctness broke and the artefact had to be understood. Vaithilingam et al. (2022) describe the surface form of this experience as acceptance without understanding, but the lived version is sharper: there was no moment at which I knew I was not learning, because in the absence of a referent there was no felt-sense of friction; the absence of friction was the problem, not the symptom. An honest essay has to engage the strongest reading of the opposite case.

The strongest counterargument is empirical: Peng et al. (2023) found that Copilot users completed an HTTP-server task approximately 55% faster than controls in a randomised trial of 95 developers, with the largest gains among less-experienced developers — and this finding should be conceded rather than disputed. The objection it grounds is that productivity gains are themselves a form of skill, and that opposing AI assistants on skill grounds is romantic about struggle. The reply distinguishes constructs. Task-completion productivity, defined as the time to finish a defined and scoped task, is what Peng et al. measure, and they measure it well. Skill acquisition, defined as the change in the developer's internal representations such that they can later debug and modify code they did not author under assistance, is a different construct, and one that the Copilot study does not measure on any time horizon longer than the task itself. The two constructs may correlate in well-designed pedagogical settings; they need not. A weaker form of the same objection comes through the calculator analogy.

The calculator analogy fails because computational off-loading and model-construction off-loading are not the same operation. A student who uses a calculator off-loads arithmetic execution; the cognitive load saved is largely extraneous to the mathematical schemas the student is building, and the calculator does not author the equation or interpret the result. Sweller's (1988) framework predicts that off-loading extraneous load can free germane load for schema construction; calculators may help. AI coding assistants, by contrast, off-load the authorship operations through which the schema is built. The analogous student would be one who hands every word problem to an oracle and copies the answer: arithmetic correct, no schema constructed. This does not entail that all AI-assisted programming pedagogy is harmful. Kazemitabaar et al. (2023) report that AI code generators can support novices when used with appropriate scaffolding, which narrows the harm-claim to unscaffolded use. What follows from this is narrower, and more useful, than "AI assistants are bad".

The defensible version of this essay's thesis is narrower than "AI assistants harm developers". It is that unscaffolded use of AI assistants during the early-acquisition window costs juniors the productive struggle that debugging skill is built from. The narrowing matters. It pins the harm to a mechanism — substitution-without-verification, in the absence of a prior model — rather than to a tool, which makes the claim testable rather than tribal. It also identifies what juniors should actually do: not abandon AI assistants, but restrict their use during the acquisition window to operations they can verify against a model they already hold, and treat any code accepted without verification as code they will not later be able to debug. The acquisition-stage mechanism extending Bainbridge (1983) is theoretical, and awaits direct empirical testing; the literature's current bridge from novice harm in programming generally to debugging skill specifically is inferential rather than established. A weaker version of this essay would have argued that AI assistants are bad. A stronger version, which the available evidence does not yet support, would have argued that the harm is permanent. The version offered here is the one I can defend: an acquisition-stage harm, with the mechanism named and the harm remediable, and worth taking seriously precisely because the productivity gains are real.
