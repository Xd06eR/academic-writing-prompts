## Revised Draft

Within a decade, short-form video platforms have moved from a curiosity to a default attention setting for undergraduates — and most accounts of their academic impact still treat the problem as one of stolen time. The displacement is real: even before TikTok existed, observational work found that the typical undergraduate sustained focus for only about six minutes before switching to a phone, with study-session length tracking inversely with the number of social-media apps installed (Rosen et al., 2013). More recent work on short-form video specifically suggests that self-reported daily TikTok use among undergraduates correlates negatively with academic engagement, an effect that persists after controlling for total screen time (Marengo et al., 2023). But framing this as displacement misses what is distinctive about short-form video as a medium.

Short-form video platforms harm academic performance not primarily because they steal time, but because their variable-reward design appears to reinforce the attention pattern that sustained study requires students to suppress. The mechanism rests on Ferster and Skinner's (1957) account of variable-ratio reinforcement, the schedule that produces the most persistent behaviour of any tested. The essay defends this claim in three steps: by establishing that short-form video use correlates with reduced sustained-focus duration beyond what screen time alone predicts, by mapping the recommendation algorithm to a variable-ratio reinforcement schedule analogous to slot-machine design, and by showing that self-reported procrastination on these platforms predicts academic outcomes more strongly than total use does. The strongest objection, that conscientiousness rather than the medium drives both heavy use and lower grades, is engaged with directly in Section 4. Section 2 develops the mechanism by returning to the operant-conditioning literature.
